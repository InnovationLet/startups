A founder hires a head of marketing from a company ten times her size. Six months later, the person has rebuilt the deck, hired an agency, and shipped nothing measurable. The startup didn't need a marketing organization. It needed one person who could write a landing page and talk to customers.

That mismatch is the most expensive pattern in early hiring, and it repeats across almost every function.

## Hire for the stage, not the title

Big-company experience often means experience managing people who manage people. Your first ten employees will do the work themselves, with no team, no budget, and no brand to lean on.

Ask candidates what they personally shipped in the last year, with their own hands. Not what their team shipped. Not what they directed. If the answer is a deck or a strategy document, keep talking.

The right early hire is usually one level below where they'd be comfortable, and hungry about it.

## Speed gets confused with fit

When you're behind on hiring, every candidate looks good enough. So you prioritize availability over quality, and the bad hire costs you twice: their output, plus the six months it takes to admit it and replace them.

Founders also over-index on interviews and under-index on work. A four-hour paid trial project tells you more than five rounds of behavioral questions ever will. You'll see how they handle ambiguity, how they write, whether they ask you anything sharp.

Two signals worth trusting: they've done something close to this exact job before at a similar stage, and they did it without being told what to do.

## The equity and role mistakes

Three things go wrong with the offer itself:

- Equity grants that vest over four years with no cliff get questioned by every future investor. One year, standard, is fine.
- Hiring a "head of" title for someone who'll be an individual contributor sets up a fall when the title doesn't survive the next round of org changes.
- Writing a vague job description attracts people who want a vague job. You'll spend weeks figuring out whether they're performing.

Also decide in writing who they report to before they sign. Ambiguity in reporting lines shows up as turf fights by employee number seven.

Give your next candidate a paid trial project instead of a fourth interview, and watch what they actually produce.
